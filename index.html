After a Georgia Hailstorm: What to Photograph on Your Roof Before You Call Anyone

A hailstorm passes over a Georgia neighborhood in twenty minutes, and from the driveway everything looks fine. The shingles are still there. Nothing is leaking — yet. But hail damage hides: dents in soft metal, bruised shingles, cracked flashing and granules knocked loose into the gutters often show no sign inside for weeks. By the time a stain appears on the ceiling, the evidence outside has already changed: the hail has melted, the debris has blown away, and a repair crew may have covered the very spots an adjuster needed to see.

That is why photographs come first — before the tarp, before the repair, and before anyone climbs up to "take a look." Your photos are the record of what the storm actually did, on the day it did it.

1. The 48-hour rule. Treat the first two days after a hailstorm as your photo window. This is not a policy deadline — check your own policy for its notice window — it is a practical one. Hailstones melt. Wind scatters the debris that shows where the storm hit hardest. Loose granules wash out of the gutters with the next rain. An adjuster compares the damage against the state of the roof before any repair, so the closer your photos are to the storm, the stronger your file. Photograph first, call second, repair third.

2. The ground-level photo set. You do not need a ladder for any of this. Walk around the house and photograph all four elevations of the roof in daylight. Then photograph the gutters and downspouts, looking for dents and for granules collecting where the water runs out. Photograph the yard: hailstones next to a coin for scale, damaged plants, dented patio furniture, and debris that fell from the roof. Then look at the soft metal — vent caps, flashing, gutters, and the fins of an outdoor air-conditioning unit — and photograph any dents close up. Each shot proves something different: the elevations show the pattern of the damage, the gutters show granule loss, the coin shot shows hail size, and the soft-metal dents show impact the shingles alone may not reveal. Keep your phone's date and location stamps on.

3. What not to climb. Stay off the roof. Wet shingles are slick, a steep pitch hides soft decking, and a bruised shingle can give way underfoot. The useful check is inside, not on top: in the attic, on a dry day, look for daylight through the boards, damp insulation, and stained decking — the same signs a roofer looks for when finding a leak. If you see any of them, note the spot and photograph it from inside. The roof itself is a roofer's job, with the right footing and the right safety gear. No photo is worth a fall.

4. The paper trail. Photos without a paper trail are half a file. Keep a single page — paper or a phone note — with the storm's date and time, the photos and where they were taken, where your policy number lives, the claim number once you have one, the date of the adjuster visit, and the name of every person you spoke to. Keep it with the photos, in one folder, until the claim is settled.

5. Three mistakes that weaken a claim. First, starting repairs before the adjuster has seen the damage. Once a shingle is replaced, the evidence of what the storm did to it is gone. Second, throwing away damaged material — torn shingles, dented flashing, broken vents. Bag the pieces and keep them with the file. Third, signing paperwork you have not read, especially anything that hands your claim proceeds to someone else or lets someone negotiate the claim on your behalf. Georgia's consumer guidance notes that negotiating an insurance claim for a homeowner is work for a licensed public adjuster, not a contractor. If a document does that, stop and read it properly before you sign.

6. What happens next. Once you have photos and a paper trail, the usual path is: you notify your insurer, an adjuster visits and inspects, you receive a written scope of what will be repaired or replaced, and your deductible applies as your policy states. If the scope does not match what your photos show, ask for a second look — and get a second opinion from a roofer who has seen the roof.
