Documenting Roof Storm Damage for an Insurance Claim: A Georgia Homeowner's Checklist

When a storm damages a roof, the repair is only half the job. The other half is the claim, and a claim runs on documentation. An adjuster who visits weeks after the storm can only judge what the evidence shows, and the evidence changes fast: hail melts, debris blows away, and a repair crew can cover the very damage the adjuster needed to see. Homeowners who keep a careful record — photos, dates, and a paper trail — almost always have an easier claim than homeowners who rely on memory. This checklist covers what to record, when to record it, and what to avoid while the claim is open.

1. Start before the storm. The strongest claim file begins with photos of the roof in good condition. Once a year, in dry weather, photograph all four sides of the roof from the ground, the gutters, and the flashing around vents and chimneys. Keep those photos in a folder with the date. When a storm hits, the same angles photographed again show the change clearly, and an adjuster can compare before and after instead of guessing. If you have records of past roof work — the year the roof went on, any repairs — keep them in the same folder.

2. The first two days after the storm. Treat the first 48 hours as your documentation window. Photograph the roof from the ground on all four sides, then the gutters and downspouts, looking for dents and for granules collecting where the water runs out. Photograph the yard: hailstones next to a coin for scale, damaged plants, dented patio furniture, and debris that fell from the roof. Photograph any soft metal — vent caps, flashing, gutters, and the fins of an outdoor air-conditioning unit — close up, since dents there show impact the shingles alone may not reveal. Keep your phone's date and location stamps on, and photograph inside too: any stain on a ceiling, damp insulation in the attic, or daylight through the boards.

3. Keep one paper trail, in one place. Photos without a paper trail are half a file. Keep a single page — paper or a phone note — with the storm's date and time, where each photo was taken, where your policy number lives, the claim number once you have one, the date of the adjuster visit, and the name of every person you spoke to, with the date of each call. Keep it with the photos, in one folder, until the claim is settled. If a roofer visits, note what they found and what they recommended, and keep any written notes they leave.

4. Temporary protection is different from repair. A tarp over a hole, or a bucket under a drip, is protection — and it is expected. Starting permanent repairs before the adjuster has seen the damage is a different matter: once a shingle is replaced, the evidence of what the storm did to it is gone. Do what is needed to stop water coming in, photograph the temporary fix, and wait for the adjuster before authorizing permanent work. The same goes for damaged material — torn shingles, dented flashing, broken vents. Bag the pieces and keep them with the file instead of throwing them away.

5. Read before you sign anything. After a storm, paperwork arrives from several directions, and some of it deserves a slow read. Be careful with any document that hands your claim proceeds to someone else, or that lets someone negotiate the claim on your behalf. In Georgia, negotiating an insurance claim for a homeowner is a job for a public adjuster, not a roofing contractor. If a document does that, stop and read it properly before you sign, and consider getting independent advice. A signature given in a hurry after a storm is hard to undo.

6. If the scope does not match your file. After the visit, the insurer sends a scope — a list of what will be repaired or replaced — and your deductible applies as your policy states. Compare that scope against your own photos and notes. If the scope misses damage your photos clearly show, ask for a second look, and get a second opinion from a roofer who has seen the roof. Keep every new document in the same folder, with dates. A claim file that is complete, dated, and organized is the best tool a homeowner has, and it costs nothing but a little time in the first two days.
