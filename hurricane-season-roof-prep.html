Hurricane Season in Georgia: How to Get Your Roof Ready Before the Storms Arrive

Hurricane season in Georgia runs from June 1 through November 30, with the busiest stretch usually falling between August and October. The coast takes the direct hit, but inland Georgia is not safe ground. Hurricane Helene in September 2024 tore roofs off homes far from the shoreline, including around Augusta, and reminded the whole state that a hurricane does not stop at the beach. A roof that is checked and tightened in late spring holds up far better than one whose loose shingles are discovered during a September storm. Most of the preparation is small, unglamorous work — and almost all of it is best done before a storm is ever named.

1. Put it on the calendar early. Do the roof work in late spring, not in the week a storm is forecast. Once a storm has a name, roofing crews are booked solid, materials run short, and the work that could have taken an afternoon becomes a wait. Pick a dry weekend in May, walk the property, and make a list. If anything on the list needs a roofer, that is the time to call — while schedules are still open and the weather is still calm.

2. Inspect from the ground first. You do not need a ladder for the first pass. Walk around the house and look up at all four sides of the roof. Watch for shingles that are missing, cracked, curled at the edges, or sitting unevenly. Look at the flashing — the metal strips around chimneys, vents, and skylights — for gaps, lifting, or rust. Check the gutters for shingle granules, which look like coarse sand and signal wear. A pair of binoculars helps with the high spots. Photograph anything that looks wrong, with the date on the photos, so you have a record of the roof's condition before the season starts.

3. Seal the edges where wind gets in. Wind damage usually starts at the edges. The eaves, the rake edges along the gable ends, and the flashing around anything that sticks through the roof are the places wind lifts first. Look for cracked or dried-out sealant, flashing that has pulled away from the wall or the roof surface, and any gap you can see daylight through from the attic. Small gaps are easy to seal when the weather is dry. In a storm, those same gaps become the entry point for wind and driven rain.

4. Deal with the trees and the yard. A healthy limb over the roof becomes a hazard in high wind, and a dead limb is a hazard in any weather. Trim back branches that hang over the roof or touch the shingles, and take down dead trees or limbs that could fall on the house. While you are at it, make a plan for the loose things in the yard — patio furniture, grills, planters, trash cans. In a hurricane they become projectiles, and the roof is usually the first thing they hit.

5. Clear the gutters and the drainage. A roof sheds water through the gutters, and the system only works if the water has somewhere to go. Clean out leaves, pine needles, and the sludge that collects in the elbows. Run a hose into each downspout and make sure water flows freely and runs away from the foundation. If a downspout dumps water right at the base of the house, add an extension. During a storm, a blocked gutter backs water up under the roof edge, which is exactly the kind of slow damage that shows up as a stain on the ceiling weeks later.

6. Check the attic, then the paperwork. In the attic, on a dry day, look for daylight coming through the boards, damp or compressed insulation, and dark stains on the decking. Those are signs of existing leaks, and a storm will make them worse. Then sort the paperwork: dated photos of the roof, the location of your insurance policy number, and the names and numbers of the people you would call after a storm. Keep a copy of the photos somewhere other than the house — a phone backup or a cloud folder — so a damaged roof does not take your evidence with it.
