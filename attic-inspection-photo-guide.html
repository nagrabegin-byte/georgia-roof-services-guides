Finding a Roof Leak from the Attic: A Photo Guide for Georgia Homeowners

A stain on the ceiling is almost never directly below the leak that caused it. Water that gets under a shingle runs along the roof decking, follows a rafter downhill, and drips off at the lowest point it can reach — sometimes several feet away from where it entered. That is why chasing a stain from inside the living room rarely works, and why the attic is the best place to start. From the attic you can see the underside of the roof, follow the water's path uphill, and photograph the evidence before anything is moved or repaired. This guide covers what to look for, what to photograph, and when to stop and call a roofer.

1. Pick the right day and the right hour. Go up on a dry day, in daylight, and go early. A Georgia attic in the afternoon can be dangerously hot in summer, so morning is the safer window. Bring a bright flashlight and your phone, and wear shoes with grip, long sleeves, and a dust mask — old insulation and dust are not kind to lungs. Step only on the joists or on boards laid across them, never on the drywall between joists. If the attic has no safe footing, or the access is a narrow scuttle with no light, do the inspection from the attic entrance with the flashlight instead of climbing in.

2. Start with the daylight test. With the attic lights off, look at the underside of the roof decking for pinpoints of daylight. Any light coming through means there is a hole, a gap, or a missing piece of decking. Photograph each spot close up and then from a few feet back, so the photo shows where on the roof the opening sits. Mark the spot with a piece of tape or a chalk mark on the nearest rafter so you can find it again. Daylight is the clearest evidence there is, and it is also the easiest to photograph.

3. Read the stains like a map. Dark stains on the decking, water marks running down a rafter, and damp or compressed insulation are the trail the water leaves. Photograph each stain with the flashlight angled across it — raking light makes a water mark stand out. Follow the stain uphill toward the ridge: the highest damp point is usually closest to where the water got in. Note whether the stain is wet to the touch or old and dry; a dry stain can be from a leak that was already repaired, while a damp one is active. Photograph rusted nail heads too — a nail that has rusted through usually means water has been reaching it for a long time.

4. Check the usual suspects. Some places leak more than others, and the photos should cover all of them. Look at the flashing around the chimney, the plumbing vents, and any skylight. Look at the valleys where two roof planes meet, and at the ridge. Look where the roof meets a wall on an addition or a dormer. Look for dark or soft spots in the decking, which can mean the wood has started to rot. If your attic has bathroom or kitchen vents, check whether they actually vent outside through the roof or just dump warm, moist air into the attic — the moisture alone can stain decking and look like a leak.

5. Photograph the insulation and the air. Damp insulation loses its loft and clumps together, and it photographs clearly. Lift a corner of a batt or move a little loose-fill aside and photograph what is underneath — that is often where a small leak hides. While you are there, look for signs of moisture in the air: a musty smell, dark spots that could be mold, or condensation on the underside of the decking in cool weather. Photograph all of it. A roofer who has seen the roof from outside will use your attic photos to connect the inside evidence to the outside cause, and good photos can save a second visit.

6. Know when to stop. Some things in an attic mean the inspection is over. If the decking sags under your hand, if a large area is wet or rotting, if you see wiring sitting in water, or if you smell gas, get out and call for help. Do not step on wet decking and do not move insulation that covers wiring. Write down everything you found, keep the photos in one folder with the date, and hand that folder to the roofer. A clear set of photos and a short written list of what you saw is the most useful thing a homeowner can give a roofing crew before anyone climbs a ladder.
